The Deterministic Previz Workflow
Blender as the Motion Lock for AI Video

Generative video models invent beautifully but obey poorly: ask for a specific camera move and a specific choreography, and every generation is a new roll of the dice. This workflow removes the dice from the parts that must not wobble — motion, camera, timing, and space are authored deterministically in Blender; only appearance is left to the model.

Extracted from a real production: the full development journey of a 30-second ninja shot, built by conversation with Claude for ~$4 of generation credit.

1 · The Workflow in Five Steps

Human 1 — State the intent, at shot level

One or two sentences of cinematography, not technology: "a 30-second single shot: a person walks down a corridor, the camera follows, then comes alive — different framings, no cuts, hero always in frame." The human owns the creative intent throughout; everything below is implementation.

Claude 2 — Formalize the prompt into a spec (not just expand it)

Before touching any tool, the agent converts the sentence into a Scene Passport: exact dimensions in metres, frame rate and range, a named camera plan (beats, lenses, holds), asset list, and — critically — acceptance criteria ("hero in frame at every sampled frame", "camera never nearer than 0.25 m to a wall"). This is the difference between prompt expansion and engineering: the spec is checkable, so every later step can be audited against it.

Claude ⇄ Higgsfield Bridge ⇄ Blender 3 — Implement deterministically in Blender

Claude drives a live Blender session through the Higgsfield Bridge (an MCP connector exposing Blender's full Python API as callable tools). It builds the set, the proxy character, the walk cycle, and the camera rig — then audits its own work: numeric sweeps (projecting the hero into camera space at 120 sample frames) and true renders it inspects visually. The output is a clean rendered guide video: every frame a ground-truth statement of where everything is and where the camera looks.

The loop — Claude + fal.ai + Blender 4 — Negotiate the silhouette (the step most people miss)

Cheap low-res test generations reveal how the video model interprets the guide's geometry — and the fixes go back into Blender, not into the text prompt. In the ninja shot: boxes generated as boxes in cloth; a smooth mannequin generated as a vinyl robot; only a connected humanoid proxy with a katana modeled onto its back generated as a ninja seen from behind. Six tests at $0.20 each bought the understanding; the geometry edits were free. The 3D proxy is part of the prompt.

Human decision → Claude + fal.ai + ffmpeg 5 — Generate the final and assemble

With the guide locked, one full-length, full-resolution generation (depth-conditioned, fixed seed) produces the shot. Segments needing a different appearance instruction — the face reveal — are generated separately from the same guide and spliced with crossfades. The human approves each spend; the agent executes and verifies.

3 · The Determinism Contract

The heart of the workflow is a clean division of authority. Depth conditioning means every generated pixel must sit on a ray the Blender camera defined — so whatever lives in geometry is locked, and whatever doesn't is negotiable.

Why this beats re-rolling text-to-video: a pure text-to-video model gives you a new camera move and a new choreography on every attempt — you can retry appearance only by also retrying everything else. Here, retries only change appearance. The shot — in the film-making sense of the word — survives every regeneration. That is what made a face-reveal splice possible: two generations, same guide, cut together without the camera ever disagreeing with itself.

4 · The Proxy Is Part of the Prompt

The least obvious and most valuable lesson. In depth-conditioned generation, the text prompt loses every argument with geometry:

proxy = boxes                 → "ninja" prompt → boxes wearing black cloth
proxy = smooth mannequin      → "ninja" prompt → glossy vinyl robot
proxy = connected humanoid    → "ninja" prompt → a person… facing the wrong way
proxy + katana on its back    → "ninja" prompt → ninja, from behind ✓

So treat the proxy as character design at depth-map resolution: connected silhouette (gaps read as separate objects), human proportions, and — for anything directional — an asymmetric feature that tells the model which side is which. A sword, a backpack, a hood-point: one honest piece of geometry outperforms three prompt rewrites, and it costs nothing.

5 · Seeing It Work

6 · Limits and Practical Notes

Close range is where depth wins too hard. Inside ~1.5 m the depth map is so dense that the character's exact surface shape is enforced — a stylized proxy stays stylized. Keep the camera ≥2.5 m from the subject, or accept stylization on close passes.

One generation, one appearance instruction. A single continuous generation applies one prompt to the whole clip; per-phase instructions ("face visible only when the camera is in front") tend to collapse to the model's bias. The fix is segmentation: generate phases separately from the same guide and splice — the locked camera makes the seams survivable.

Length costs temporal resolution. Models cap frames per call (241 at 16 fps for wan-vace); longer shots auto-downsample and interpolate back. Plan beats so nothing critical depends on 60 ms timing.

Budget shape: iterate at 480p (cents), commit at 720p once (dollars). The ninja shot: six tests $1.20, reference image $0.003, final passes $2.80 — ≈$4 total, with all 3D work and editing free and local.

Generalizes freely. Any DCC that renders a clean animation works in the Blender seat; any depth- or pose-conditioned video model works in the fal.ai seat. The contract — geometry locks structure, prompts paint appearance — is the workflow, not the specific tools.
